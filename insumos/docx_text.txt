Executive Summary
Mexico is at a formative stage in green hydrogen development. While the country has strong renewable resources and an established industrial base that already consumes hydrogen, most green hydrogen projects remain at concept or feasibility stage. Institutional responsibilities are dispersed and Mexico currently lacks dedicated hydrogen legislation, although initial hydrogen guidelines were issued in 2024.
Hydrogen markets globally are often constrained by common structural barriers including high production costs, revenue uncertainty, regulatory gaps, and insufficient infrastructure. Countries that have progressed beyond pilots typically do so by gradually resolving these constraints through clear leadership, coordinated regulation, and a sequenced policy mix.
This report presents an international benchmarking assessment designed to support Mexico’s Ministry of Energy, SENER (Secretaría de Energía), as it develops the country’s first National Renewable Hydrogen Plan and considers the sequencing, coordination and policy alignment. Eleven markets of different maturities were assessed against a qualitative benchmarking framework that covered three core dimensions:
Governance & Institutional Arrangements: Leadership structures, coordination, strategic anchoring, monitoring, and stakeholder engagement.
Legal & Regulatory Foundations: Definitions, permitting frameworks, certification systems, and remaining regulatory gaps.
Policy Levers: Fiscal incentives, public funding, revenue support mechanisms, standards and certification, and infrastructureenabling policies.
The eleven countries assessed were agreed with SENER and included: Germany, Netherlands and the United Kingdom, which can generally be considered advanced hydrogen markets in the international context; Japan, Australia, South Korea and China which can be considered to have medium maturity hydrogen markets; India and Chile, which can be considered to have emerging hydrogen markets; and Colombia and Peru which are early stage and can be considered ‘peer’ countries to Mexico.
The results of this assessment provide a structured, evidence-based overview of how these countries have approached the governance, regulatory, and policy foundations needed to enable renewable (green) hydrogen markets. Key findings include:
Governance maturity is the strongest differentiator between pilot and scaling markets: Countries that have moved beyond pilot projects embed hydrogen within binding national strategies, ensure clear institutional leadership, maintain formal interagency coordination and policy coherence.
Mature markets (e.g., Germany, UK) integrate hydrogen comprehensively across their energy, climate, and industrial policies, resulting in a coherent and mutually reinforcing policy framework.
Earlystage markets (e.g., Peru, Colombia) rely on fragmented mandates and informal coordination.
Regulatory certainty evolves incrementally, not all at once: even advanced markets display existing gaps. At early stages, the most critical regulations to address are those that reduce investor risk.
Advanced markets have clear definitions, operational certification schemes, and hydrogen dedicated regulatory structures.
Most countries begin by adapting existing energy and environmental laws before developing hydrogen specific regulation. Dedicated hydrogen regulation typically appear only at later stages (e.g., South Korea).
Policy levers matter less individually and more in how they are sequenced: Successful hydrogen markets employ policy support in a staged progression:
Earlystage: fiscal incentives, pilots, public funding.
Scaling stage: revenue stabilisation mechanisms (e.g. public tenders, contracts for differences).
Advanced stage: coordinated infrastructure investments and systemintegration policies.
Countries that fail to progress beyond pilots often rely on isolated levers without evolving toward revenuerisk absorption or infrastructure coordination.
Infrastructure planning becomes essential once multiple projects emerge: Countries with industrial clusters or export ambitions prioritise shared hydrogen infrastructure (pipelines, ports, hubs). Markets that do not coordinate infrastructure face cost inflation and stalled integration.
Policies focussed exclusively on production incentives are often insufficient for scaling up: To ensure sustainable growth, demand-side measures that guarantee offtake are equally crucial. Securing long-term demand through targeted policies helps create a stable market environment, reduces investment risks, and encourages the development of comprehensive hydrogen value chains,
Across the benchmark countries, the central finding is that sequencing and alignment, rather than any single policy instrument is most likely to determine whether hydrogen development advances from strategy to bankable investment. Countries that combine clear institutional leadership, progressively refined regulatory frameworks, and evolving policy support that adapts to market maturity are consistently more successful in progressing beyond pilotlevel activity.
The next phase for this work under the UK’s Green Cities Infrastructure and Energy Programme will assess Mexico’s existing regulatory and policy framework and examine potential models for cross-government coordination, drawing on international experience and external stakeholder’s perspective to support SENER in strengthening the governance framework for green hydrogen development in Mexico.
Introduction 
This report presents an international benchmarking assessment of the governance, regulatory and policy frameworks that countries have applied to enable the development of renewable (green) hydrogen markets. It is intended to support the Government of Mexico, and in particular the Ministry of Energy (SENER, Secretaría de Energía), as it develops the institutional and policy foundations for a future National Renewable Hydrogen Policy.
The assessment is analytical and descriptive in nature. It does not recommend specific policy instruments or seek to replicate international models. Instead, it provides a structured, evidence-based reference on how green hydrogen markets have been organised in practice across different jurisdictions, how responsibilities and frameworks have been allocated, and how policy design and sequencing influences whether projects progress beyond early announcements toward investment and delivery. This positioning is fully consistent with the scope defined in the Terms of Reference and complements parallel technical work on industrial applications and project pipelines.
The report is organised in four parts. It first sets out Mexico’s current national context for renewable hydrogen, focusing on the policy, institutional and market conditions that define the starting point for development. It then examines the green hydrogen sector more broadly, drawing on international experience to outline the structural barriers that commonly constrain deployment. Building on this context, the report applies a consistent analytical framework across international benchmark countries at different stages of market maturity, from early planning through to scaling and later-stage deployment. The framework focuses on three core dimensions, while also recognising the role of infrastructure planning and system integration:
Governance and institutional arrangements, examining which public entities lead hydrogen policy, how responsibilities are allocated across ministries, regulators and public agencies, and how coordination is managed across the hydrogen value chain.
Legal and regulatory foundations, assessing the development of hydrogen-specific legislation, permitting frameworks, sustainability criteria and certification systems, and the degree of regulatory clarity provided to developers and investors.
Policy levers, reviewing the range of instruments applied to support deployment, including fiscal and tax incentives, public funding and CAPEX co-financing, market and revenue support mechanisms, standards and certification, and infrastructure-enabling policies.
Using this framework, the report assesses eleven benchmark countries, selected jointly with SENER, spanning advanced, scaling and peer hydrogen markets, to compare how governance arrangements, regulatory frameworks and policy instruments differ across stages of market maturity and to identify approaches relevant to Mexico’s policy development.
Drawing on this comparative analysis, the report presents a consolidated set of Key Findings and conclusions that draw together findings across governance, regulation and policy design. These findings highlight how different combinations and sequencing of approaches shape market outcomes at different stages of development and clarify the conditions under which countries have moved from strategy and pilots to investable, bankable projects. The analysis provides a grounded reference to inform policy dialogue with SENER and support the next phase of decision-making as Mexico considers how international experience can inform the design and sequencing of its renewable hydrogen policy and associated enabling frameworks.
Renewable Hydrogen in Mexico
Mexico is at a formative stage in the development of a renewable (green) hydrogen sector. The country combines strong renewable resource potential with an established industrial base that already consumes hydrogen, alongside growing interest from private developers. At the same time, the current policy, regulatory and institutional environment reflects an early-stage market, where most announced projects remain at concept or feasibility level and system-level enabling conditions are still evolving.
This section provides an overview of Mexico’s national context relevant to green hydrogen development, focusing on the energy and climate policy landscape, the role of hydrogen in existing industrial activity, the emerging project pipeline, and the current institutional and regulatory setting. This section is descriptive in nature and is solely intended to establish context rather than evaluate policy effectiveness or market performance.
3.1 National Energy and Climate Context
Mexico is undergoing a gradual energy transition within a system that remains heavily reliant on fossil fuels. Approximately three-quarters of electricity generation continues to be supplied by fossil sources, primarily natural gas, while low-carbon generation accounts for a significantly smaller share compared to regional and global averages. In response, the Federal Government of Mexico has articulated objectives to expand clean electricity generation through national energy planning instruments and its updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), which identifies industrial decarbonisation as a priority area.
Within this context, renewable hydrogen is increasingly considered as a potential complement to direct electrification, particularly for sectors where electrification is technically or economically challenging. International experience suggests that hydrogen deployment is most relevant in hard-to-abate sectors, such as refining, chemicals, fertilisers, mining and selected industrial processes, rather than in applications where direct electricity use is more efficient. Mexico’s exploration of green hydrogen aligns with this global pattern, positioning hydrogen as a potential long-term option rather than an immediate substitute across the energy system.
Mexico’s renewable resource endowment, particularly in solar and wind, is widely recognised as a structural advantage for potential green hydrogen production. Several regions combine high-quality renewable resources with proximity to industrial demand, transport corridors and ports, creating conditions that are commonly associated with early hydrogen clusters in other countries. However, as observed internationally, resource potential alone does not translate into deployment without supportive institutional, regulatory and market frameworks.
3.2 Role of hydrogen in Mexico’s industrial sector
Hydrogen is already used across several strategic industrial sectors in Mexico, most notably in oil refining, chemicals, mining, metals and fertiliser production. At present, this hydrogen is almost entirely fossil-based, produced primarily through steam methane reforming or similar conventional processes. Existing hydrogen use therefore reflects established industrial demand rather than low-carbon deployment.
From a green hydrogen perspective, these sectors represent natural entry points for future substitution. International experience indicates that incumbent hydrogen users are often among the first adopters of renewable hydrogen, as they possess existing technical capabilities, operational familiarity and, in some cases, concentrated demand. Recent sectoral analyses conducted as part of Mexico’s Clean Hydrogen Industrial Strategy estimate indicative, pilot-level substitution potential of over 390,000 tonnes of hydrogen per year across key sectors. This figure reflects technical potential rather than confirmed demand or committed offtake and should be interpreted accordingly.
Geographically, Mexico’s industrial activity and energy infrastructure are concentrated in a limited number of states, including Veracruz, Tamaulipas, Nuevo León, Sonora, Baja California and Durango. Several of these regions also exhibit strong renewable resource availability, creating opportunities for early co-location of renewable generation, electrolysis and industrial demand. International evidence suggests that such clustering can reduce integration costs and infrastructure requirements, particularly at early stages of market development.

3.4 Institutional and regulatory context for hydrogen
Mexico does not currently have a hydrogen-specific legal or regulatory framework. Hydrogen-related activities are instead governed through existing sectoral laws and cross-cutting regulatory regimes, depending on project configuration, production pathways and end uses.
In the absence of dedicated hydrogen legislation, green hydrogen projects in Mexico may be subject to a combination of the following legal frameworks:
Hydrocarbons Law (Ley de Hidrocarburos): Applies where hydrogen is integrated into refining, fuel processing or activities linked to regulated hydrocarbon value chains.
Electricity Industry Law (Ley de la Industria Eléctrica): Governs renewable electricity generation, grid interconnection and electricity supply arrangements relevant to electrolysis-based hydrogen production.
General Law on Climate Change (Ley General de Cambio Climático): Provides the overarching climate policy context and frames national mitigation objectives relevant to low-carbon hydrogen development.
Environmental, water-use and industrial safety requirements are addressed through cross-cutting regulatory regimes rather than hydrogen-specific rules. As a result, developers must comply with multiple legal and administrative processes in parallel, increasing regulatory complexity at early stages of project development. This reliance on existing legislation is consistent with international experience in early-stage hydrogen markets, where dedicated legal frameworks typically emerge only as deployment begins to scale.
In 2024, the Ministry of Energy issued Hydrogen Guidelines (Lineamientos en Materia de Hidrógeno), which outline general principles for sector development and represent an initial step toward a more dedicated framework. At present, however, hydrogen-specific permitting regimes, sustainability criteria and certification systems are not yet in place, and projects must navigate multiple regulatory processes depending on their design.
From an institutional perspective, responsibility for hydrogen-related activities in Mexico is distributed across multiple federal, state and local authorities, reflecting the cross-sectoral nature of the hydrogen value chain.

Global Sector Dynamics 
Green hydrogen is at an early stage of development globally, with deployment concentrated in pilot projects, demonstration initiatives and a limited number of early commercial applications. While interest in hydrogen has grown rapidly across regions, progress has been uneven, reflecting differences in sectoral demand, infrastructure readiness and the maturity of enabling policy and regulatory frameworks. Mexico’s hydrogen sector exhibits many of the characteristics observed in other early-stage markets, providing a relevant reference point for examining global deployment patterns and constraints.
4.1 Global hydrogen value chain and deployment patterns
Globally, the green hydrogen value chain spans renewable electricity generation, hydrogen production through electrolysis, storage and transport, and end-use applications across industry, energy and transport. International experience shows that these elements rarely develop simultaneously, particularly in early markets.
Deployment has typically begun with pilot and demonstration projects focused on hydrogen production and limited end-use applications. As markets mature, attention shifts toward system integration, shared infrastructure and coordinated development across multiple projects and users. Countries that have progressed beyond pilots have generally addressed value-chain constraints through coordinated approaches rather than isolated, project-level interventions.
In terms of applications, green hydrogen is primarily targeted at sectors where direct electrification is not technically feasible or economically efficient. These include existing hydrogen-consuming industries such as refining, ammonia and chemicals, selected industrial processes, heavy transport and, in some cases, power system balancing. International experience consistently indicates that hydrogen functions as a targeted decarbonisation option rather than a universal solution.
4.2 Common barriers to green hydrogen deployment
Across countries and regions, green hydrogen deployment is constrained by a consistent set of structural barriers. These barriers have been observed across markets with varying resource endowments, policy ambition and stages of development.
Revenue uncertainty and demand riskGreen hydrogen remains materially more expensive than fossil-based alternatives in most applications. In the absence of predictable long-term revenues or committed offtake, projects face high market risk, limiting bankability and access to project finance. Where demand and revenue risks are not addressed, project pipelines tend to stall at announcement or feasibility stage.
Regulatory uncertainty and evolving standardsIn many jurisdictions, hydrogen-specific definitions, certification systems and permitting regimes are either absent or still evolving. This creates regulatory and compliance uncertainty for developers and financiers, increases transaction costs and slows project development, particularly for first-of-a-kind initiatives.
High production costs and capital intensityGreen hydrogen production requires substantial upfront investment in electrolysers, renewable generation and associated infrastructure. Under current market conditions, production costs typically exceed those of conventional hydrogen by a significant margin. High capital intensity constrains early deployment and increases reliance on public support or balance-sheet financing.
Infrastructure gaps and system integration challengesHydrogen deployment depends on dedicated infrastructure for production, storage, transport and distribution. In early markets, the absence of shared infrastructure forces projects to rely on bespoke, project-specific solutions, increasing costs and execution risk. As multiple projects emerge, system integration and infrastructure planning become increasingly critical.
Coordination and execution challengesHydrogen value chains involve multiple actors across energy, industry, environment, water, transport and trade. Where institutional roles are unclear or coordination mechanisms are weak, decision-making can be delayed, permitting processes can become fragmented and project timelines can be extended.
These sectoral barriers provide a common reference point for understanding green hydrogen development across countries. International experience shows that differences in deployment outcomes are shaped less by the presence of individual instruments and more by how governance arrangements, regulatory foundations and policy levers are structured and sequenced to address these constraints. The analytical framework applied in the sections that follow examines how different countries have responded to these barriers through alternative institutional, regulatory and policy approaches.
Methodology and Analytical Framework
This assessment applies a structured qualitative benchmarking methodology to compare how different countries have designed and implemented governance arrangements, legal and regulatory frameworks, and policy instruments to support the development of renewable hydrogen markets. The methodology is based on a systematic review of publicly available primary sources, including national strategies, legislation, regulatory instruments, official guidance, programme documentation and government publications, complemented by secondary analysis from international organisations and rigorous analytical literature. 
A common analytical framework was designed and applied consistently across all benchmark countries to enable comparison across the different stages of market maturity. The approach is descriptive and comparative rather than evaluative: it documents how policy frameworks are structured and sequenced in practice and how these evolve as markets develop, without scoring, ranking or assessing the effectiveness of individual policies.
The rationale for the selection of the analytical dimensions applied in this framework is set out below.
5.1 Rationale for the Analytical Framework
Alignment with the Terms of Reference
The analytical framework applied in this assessment is directly aligned with the scope and objectives defined in the Terms of Reference (ToR) for Deliverable 2.1. The ToR explicitly require an international benchmarking assessment examining institutional arrangements, regulatory structures, and policy mechanisms relevant to renewable hydrogen market development.
The three-dimensional framework adopted in this report, (i) Governance and Institutional Arrangements, (ii) Legal and Regulatory Foundations, and (iii) Policy Levers, provides a structured approach to systematically address these requirements.
Governance and Institutional Arrangements respond to the ToR requirement to analyse leadership models, inter-agency coordination and institutional responsibilities across the hydrogen value chain.
Legal and Regulatory Foundations address the need to assess regulatory clarity, permitting regimes, sustainability criteria and certification systems relevant to hydrogen deployment.
Policy Levers respond to the requirement to examine the economic and market instruments used internationally to enable renewable hydrogen development.
By structuring the benchmarking assessment across these three dimensions, the framework ensures comprehensive coverage of the institutional, regulatory and economic conditions specified in the ToR, while enabling consistent cross-country comparison across different stages of market maturity.
Methodological and Theoretical Rationale for the Three-Dimensional Framework
The selection of these three dimensions is grounded in established economic theory, technology deployment literature, and methodological approaches used by leading international energy institutions.
International analyses of emerging clean technology markets, including those conducted by the International Energy Agency (IEA), the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), the World Bank, and the OECD, consistently identify a recurring set of structural barriers that determine whether technologies progress from pilot phases to large-scale deployment. Across these assessments, three categories of constraints are repeatedly highlighted: coordination and institutional fragmentation; cost gaps and capital intensity; and revenue, demand and market-formation risks. 
These barriers are particularly acute in hydrogen markets, where multi-actor value chains, infrastructure interdependencies and regulatory uncertainty interact to delay investment and scale-up. Drawing on this body of international evidence, the benchmarking framework applied in this section organises the enabling conditions around the following three barrier categories:
Coordination and Institutional BarriersEmerging sectors such as renewable hydrogen involve multiple ministries, regulators, system operators and industrial actors. Economic theory recognises coordination failures and fragmented mandates as key barriers in multi-actor markets. Governance clarity and institutional alignment reduce transaction costs, accelerate permitting, and improve policy coherence.
Regulatory Uncertainty and Investment RiskInvestment theory emphasises that regulatory uncertainty increases risk premiums and delays capital allocation. Clear definitions, permitting regimes, safety standards and certification systems reduce uncertainty, improve predictability, and enable access to finance. International hydrogen assessments consistently identify regulatory clarity as a precondition for bankability.
Economic and Market FailuresRenewable hydrogen markets face cost gaps relative to incumbent fossil alternatives, demand uncertainty, and infrastructure externalities. Economic theory on market failures, including learning-by-doing, positive externalities and network effects, demonstrates the need for targeted public intervention through fiscal instruments, revenue stabilisation mechanisms and infrastructure coordination.

For example:

Together, these three dimensions reflect not only the scope of the project but also internationally recognised methodological practice for assessing emerging clean energy markets.
Scope clarificationThe benchmarking presented in this section focuses specifically on institutional, regulatory and policy enabling conditions for green hydrogen market development. It does not include detailed techno-economic modelling (e.g., levelised cost of hydrogen – LCOH analysis), project-by-project bankability assessments, or quantitative scoring and ranking of countries. These dimensions fall outside the scope of this deliverable and are either addressed through other analytical workstreams or require separate, dedicated modelling exercises. The objective of this section is therefore descriptive and comparative, rather than financial or prescriptive.
5.2 Analytical Framework
Countries have adopted a wide range of approaches to enable the development of renewable (green) hydrogen markets, reflecting differences in institutional arrangements, regulatory foundations and policy priorities. These approaches have evolved as markets have moved from early planning and pilots toward deployment at scale. A structured comparative framework is therefore required to examine how governance, regulation and policy design differ across jurisdictions and how these differences relate to observed market outcomes. For the purpose of this assessment, the three following core dimensions were considered.  
Dimension 1: Governance and Institutional Arrangements
Considers which public entities lead hydrogen policy, how responsibilities are distributed across ministries, regulators and public agencies, and how coordination is managed across the value chain.
Dimension 2: Legal and Regulatory Foundations
Examines the presence or absence of hydrogen-specific legislation, permitting regimes, sustainability criteria and certification systems.
Dimension 3: Policy Levers
Assesses the potential effectiveness of five core policy levers* that international experience and relevant literature have traditionally maintained are central to green hydrogen deployment. 
The policy levers that have been assessed in this report are (1) fiscal and tax incentives; (2) direct public funding and CAPEX co-financing; (3) market and revenue support mechanisms; (4) standards, certification and regulation; and (5) infrastructure-enabling policies.

6.2 Governance and Institutional Arrangements
Across the sample of eleven benchmark countries, differences in hydrogen deployment outcomes are strongly associated with how governance arrangements are structured and coordinated. Countries that have progressed beyond pilot stages tend to exhibit clearer institutional leadership, formal coordination mechanisms and explicit strategic anchoring for hydrogen within broader energy and industrial policy. By contrast, early-stage markets typically rely on fragmented mandates and informal coordination, which constrains delivery even where policy ambition exists.
In this assessment, ‘governance’ refers to the institutional framework that underpins hydrogen development, including how responsibilities are allocated across public bodies and how coordination is managed across the value chain. While institutional setups and lead authorities vary across jurisdictions, international experience points to a set of recurring governance practices that support strategic alignment, investment confidence and coordinated delivery.

6.3 Legal and Regulatory Foundations
Legal and regulatory frameworks for hydrogen tend to evolve incrementally as markets mature. International experience indicates that early deployment is shaped less by the existence of comprehensive hydrogen-specific legislation than by whether core regulatory issues, particularly those affecting safety, permitting and eligibility, are sufficiently resolved to provide confidence for early investment. Differences observed across countries therefore reflect sequencing choices and regulatory prioritisation, rather than the simple presence or absence of regulation.
For the purpose of this assessment, we define ‘legal and regulatory foundations’ as the regulatory architecture that underpins how hydrogen projects are classified, permitted, certified and supervised. This includes the existence of usable definitions for green hydrogen, the presence or absence of hydrogen-specific regulation, the development of certification and sustainability standards, and the identification of regulatory gaps that affect project development.
This assessment examines how countries have structured their legal and regulatory frameworks for hydrogen, and how these frameworks differ in terms of clarity, maturity and alignment with international practices.

These levers operate alongside governance arrangements and regulatory frameworks, shaping how hydrogen initiatives progress from policy intent toward implementation across different stages of market maturity.

Key Findings 
This section presents the key findings emerging from the comparative benchmarking analysis across governance, legal and regulatory foundations, and policy levers. The findings synthesise observed patterns across the benchmark countries, highlighting how these dimensions interact and evolve together at different stages of hydrogen market development. They are analytical rather than evaluative in nature and do not assess the effectiveness of individual policies in isolation or imply direct causality between specific instruments and deployment outcomes.
7.1 Governance and Institutional Arrangements
Across benchmark countries, governance maturity is characterised by the presence of binding strategic anchors, clear institutional leadership and formal coordination mechanisms, which together provide the stability and delivery capacity required for hydrogen markets to progress beyond pilots.
Strategic Anchoring and Policy Coherence: Countries that scale hydrogen anchor it in binding national strategies and embed it across energy, climate, and industrial policy, creating institutional coherence and priority-setting.
Binding strategies create institutional consistency and durability: Where strategies are embedded in policy cycles, mandates are clearer and more stable (e.g., Germany) than in mission-based or evolving setups (e.g., India).
Deep policy integration separates scaling markets from pilot markets: Hydrogen is embedded across energy, climate, and industrial policy in mature systems (e.g., UK, Germany) but referenced selectively in early-stage ones (e.g., Colombia, Peru).
National hydrogen strategies anchor institutional alignment across government: In all countries, strategies organize action; in mature systems they are binding (e.g., Germany, UK) while in early-stage markets they mainly signal intent (e.g., Peru).
Institutional Leadership and Delivery Capacity: Scaling markets combine clear leadership with formal coordination and monitoring mechanisms; early-stage markets remain constrained by diffuse mandates and weak delivery capacity.
Hybrid energy–industry leadership dominates in scaling hydrogen markets: Mature systems span energy and industrial authorities (e.g., Germany, Japan), while early-stage countries rely on energy-only leadership (e.g., Peru, Colombia).
Formal coordination mechanisms distinguish mature from developing governance: Established markets use formal cross-ministerial coordination (e.g., Germany, UK), while early-stage systems depend on informal or project-based coordination (e.g., Peru).
Monitoring and evaluation capacity increases with governance maturity: Structured tracking frameworks exist in mature markets (e.g., Germany), whereas early-stage systems show limited or no systematic monitoring (e.g., Peru).
Market Engagement and External Alignment: As hydrogen markets mature, governance shifts from consultation and pilots toward institutionalised engagement with industry and structured international cooperation.
Stakeholder engagement evolves from consultation to institutionalized participation: Early-stage systems focus on pilots and consultations (e.g., Chile, Peru), while mature markets sustain ongoing engagement with industry and research (e.g., Germany, Netherlands).
International cooperation is embedded where hydrogen is export-oriented: Export- and supply-chain-led countries institutionalize cooperation (e.g., Netherlands, Japan), while early-stage markets engage selectively (e.g., Peru).
7.2 Legal and Regulatory Foundations
International experience indicates that regulatory certainty for hydrogen emerges progressively, with market development shaped less by the absence of gaps than by whether core regulatory issues affecting safety, permitting and eligibility are sufficiently resolved to support early investment.
Gaps and Investor Risk: The differentiator across countries is not gaps, but how material they are.
All markets retain gaps, even regulatory leaders: Advanced jurisdictions still face unresolved issues around scaling and system integration (e.g., UK, Germany).
Early-stage markets face structural regulatory uncertainty: Missing safety, permitting, and certification frameworks raise project risk and delays (e.g., Peru, Colombia).
Regulatory clarity improves through iteration, not upfront design: Countries refine rules progressively as projects advance and risks become clearer (e.g., India, Australia).
Regulatory Sequencing and Market Maturity: Hydrogen regulation evolves sequentially, tightening only as markets move from pilots to scale.
Clear definitions anchor markets before regulation deepens: Countries first define emissions thresholds, renewable electricity sourcing, and accounting rules, then apply them across support schemes and certification
Embedded regulation dominates early and mid-stage marketsMost countries adapt existing energy and industrial laws before considering hydrogen-specific frameworks (e.g., Germany, Netherlands, UK).
Dedicated hydrogen laws appear only at advanced scaleStandalone hydrogen legislation emerges later, reflecting complexity rather than enabling early deployment (e.g., South Korea).
7.3 Policy Levers
The benchmarking shows that policy levers influence deployment outcomes primarily through their sequencing and interaction over time, with early-stage instruments enabling entry and learning, and later-stage mechanisms addressing revenue risk, infrastructure coordination and system integration.
Policy Sequencing and Market Maturity Logic: Early-stage policy focuses on enabling entry, while advanced policy focuses on system orchestration. Misaligned sequencing leads to stalled pilots.
Sequencing matters more than any single policy lever: No single instrument explains success. Outcomes depend on how levers are sequenced as markets evolve from pilots to scale.
Policy mix sophistication increases with market maturity: Early-stage countries rely on isolated levers, while mature markets deploy coordinated policy packages that evolve with scale (e.g., Peru vs. Germany).
Fiscal and tax incentives are entry tools, not scale drivers: Fiscal incentives primarily support early experimentation and learning, but do not unlock large-scale investment on their own (e.g., Colombia, Peru).
Risk Allocation and Market Creation Mechanisms: Hydrogen does not scale until someone absorbs market risk. Countries choose different risk anchors, but all successful ones make that choice explicit.
Revenue support mechanisms separate pilot markets from scaling markets: Countries that move beyond pilots consistently introduce revenue stabilization mechanisms to close the cost gap and enable bankability (e.g., Germany, UK vs. Chile).
Advanced markets explicitly socialize price and demand risk: In mature ecosystems, governments absorb early market risk to convert policy ambition into bankable cashflows (e.g., Germany H2Global).
Demand-side anchoring can substitute for production subsidies: Some markets prioritize guaranteed demand through regulated offtake rather than direct production support, especially where domestic production is constrained (e.g., South Korea).
System Enablers and Structural Readiness: Once scale is visible, coordination beats optimization. Standards and infrastructure determine whether growth is efficient or fragmented
Standards and certification enable markets but do not drive deployment: Certification frameworks reduce uncertainty and unlock finance but only deliver deployment impact when combined with financial or market levers (e.g., EU RFNBO rules).
Infrastructure policy becomes critical once projects scale in parallel: As multiple projects emerge, infrastructure coordination shifts from optional to essential to manage integration risk and system costs (e.g., Netherlands, Australia).
Infrastructure-led strategies dominate in clustered or export-oriented markets: Countries with strong industrial clusters or export ambitions prioritise shared backbones and hubs early to position hydrogen as a strategic system asset (e.g., Netherlands ports, Australia exports). 
When considered collectively, the findings indicate that differences in renewable hydrogen deployment across countries are less strongly associated with any single governance model, regulatory instrument or policy lever than with how these elements are combined and sequenced as markets evolve. Across the benchmark countries, hydrogen markets appear more likely to progress beyond pilot stages where institutional responsibilities are clearly anchored, regulatory frameworks provide sufficient clarity to support early investment, and policy support evolves as scale and system complexity increase. Conversely, where governance arrangements, regulatory foundations and policy levers remain fragmented or misaligned, deployment has tended to remain limited despite stated ambition or favourable resource conditions. 
These cross-cutting patterns provide the basis for the concluding observations set out in the following section.
Conclusion
This international benchmarking assessment has examined how countries at different stages of market maturity have structured their governance arrangements, legal and regulatory foundations, and policy levers to enable the development of renewable hydrogen markets. The analysis has been undertaken to support SENER, as well as the Government of Mexico more broadly, as it considers the institutional and policy foundations for a future National Renewable Hydrogen Policy, and is intended to provide an analytical reference grounded in observed international experience rather than a prescriptive blueprint.
The benchmarking suggests that progress beyond pilot and announcement stages tends to be shaped less by the presence of individual policy instruments and more by how governance, regulation and policy support are combined and sequenced over time. 
For countries that have moved toward investment and delivery, hydrogen development is typically anchored in relatively clear institutional leadership and coordination, supported by regulatory frameworks that provide sufficient clarity to enable early investment, and complemented by policy levers that evolve as markets mature. Where these elements appear fragmented or misaligned, project pipelines are more likely to stall despite strong resource potential or policy ambition.
The various international experiences considered as part of this assessment also indicate that hydrogen policy frameworks generally evolve incrementally. Early-stage markets often rely heavily on existing legal and institutional structures, with hydrogen-specific regulation, certification systems and infrastructure planning emerging progressively as deployment scales and complexity increases. Similarly, policy support commonly shifts over time from early-stage funding and fiscal incentives toward mechanisms that address revenue risk, infrastructure coordination and system integration. These patterns highlight the importance of sequencing and interaction between policy domains, rather than the early adoption of comprehensive or highly prescriptive frameworks.
For SENER, this assessment helps to situate the prevailing national conditions within a broader set of global dynamics observed in the eleven benchmark countries at the different stages of market maturity. While the assessment does not assume that a single model or outcome is universally applicable, the findings presented in this report offer a structured evidence base to inform policy dialogue by clarifying how different countries have organised responsibilities, addressed regulatory uncertainty and applied policy support at comparable stages of development. 
In doing so, this report establishes a structured basis for interpreting international experience by examining how governance arrangements, legal and regulatory foundations, and policy levers interact and evolve across different stages of market development. This provides a foundation for considering how such experience could be selectively adapted and appropriately sequenced over time as Mexico advances the development of its first renewable hydrogen policy and associated enabling frameworks.

The next phase of this work package under the UK’s Green Cities, Infrastructure and Energy Programme will analyse Mexico’s existing regulatory and policy framework and synthesise policy options. This will be validated through an internal technical validation workshop with GCIEP, SENER and the FCDO Post, alongside stakeholder consultations, to further support SENER in its journey to establish a governance framework for green hydrogen growth in Mexico.

Australia
Australia operates under a federal–state governance model. National authorities set strategic direction and funding frameworks, while state governments lead hydrogen hub development, permitting and infrastructure delivery. Public agencies such as ARENA administer grants and innovation funding, and the Clean Energy Regulator (CER) oversees renewable certification and emissions accounting relevant to hydrogen.
Hydrogen certification and regulatory frameworks are still evolving, particularly to ensure export market credibility. Institutional roles, however, are clearly delineated across federal and state levels. Australia is positioned at a scaling, export-oriented stage, with governance designed to support large, hub-based projects linked to ports and renewable resource zones.

Renewable Energy Context

Australia’s high-quality solar and wind resources, combined with designated renewable energy zones near ports, provide strong structural conditions for export-oriented green hydrogen production.
Hydrogen Value Chain Structure
Australia follows an export-led, hub-based hydrogen value chain model:
Production:Large-scale electrolysis projects located near renewable resource zones and connected to dedicated transmission or behind-the-meter arrangements; production is oriented both to domestic industrial use and export (ammonia, liquid hydrogen).

Transport / Distribution:Transport infrastructure organised around hub-and-spoke models linking renewable zones, ports and industrial clusters; port terminals and shipping logistics are central for export pathways rather than a national hydrogen pipeline backbone in the near term.
Applications:Primary focus on export markets (ammonia, shipping fuels) alongside targeted domestic industrial decarbonisation in mining, chemicals and heavy industry clusters; potential future use in power and heavy transport as domestic markets mature.

China
China’s hydrogen development is embedded within a planning-led, multi-level governance system. Strategic direction is set by central authorities, notably the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) and the National Energy Administration (NEA). Provincial governments and state-owned enterprises (SOEs) are responsible for project execution, infrastructure development and financing, typically within industrial parks and renewable energy bases.
Rather than a standalone hydrogen market law, governance is anchored in national and provincial planning frameworks and sectoral regulations. This places China in an industrial-scale, execution-oriented pathway, driven by administrative coordination and SOE investment rather than market-based contracting.

Market and revenue support 
mechanisms
H2Global operates as a government-backed procurement mechanism using Contracts-for-Difference–type (CfD-type) contracts, where hydrogen producers are awarded long-term supply agreements through competitive auctions
Under this model, producers bid the price at which they can supply hydrogen or hydrogen-based derivatives, while the government commits to covering the difference between this contracted price and the prevailing market resale price
Hydrogen is resold to the market at market-linked prices, and the public sector absorbs the price gap per unit delivered, effectively removing price volatility and demand risk
This structure converts policy support into predictable, contract-backed revenues, enabling project development and financing without exposure to merchant market conditions
